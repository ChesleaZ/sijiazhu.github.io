Mathematical Foundations of Machine Learning under Uncertainty

Undergraduate research, 2023–2025. Five papers.

Learning from vague or incomplete information starts with one question: how different are two uncertain objects? During my undergraduate studies I built new ways to answer it for several modern models of uncertainty, proved that they behave as a distance or divergence should, and used them for recognition, diagnosis, clustering, image segmentation and decision making.

Motivation

Real data is rarely clean. A patient's symptoms only partly match a disease, an expert hesitates between two ratings, and a pixel sits on the border between two regions. Fuzzy sets handle this by giving each element a degree of membership. Later models add more detail. Intuitionistic fuzzy sets add a degree of non-membership. Picture fuzzy sets add neutrality. Pythagorean and Fermatean fuzzy sets relax the constraint on membership and non-membership, so they can describe evaluations that the earlier models cannot (figure below). Complex-valued versions add a second, periodic component.

Every richer model needs its own way to compare objects, and that is where the gap was. Some of these models had no distance measure at all. Others had measures that break down on hard cases. In one pattern-recognition example from paper [1], several existing measures give an unknown sample exactly the same distance to two different patterns, so they cannot decide between them, while the new α-divergences tell the two apart. Any classifier, clustering method or decision rule built on such a measure inherits the problem.

Contributions

A fundamental challenge in machine learning is how to quantify the difference between two uncertain objects. In five papers, we developed new divergence measures for uncertain data and established their theoretical properties. We then provided a mathematical foundation for applications in pattern recognition, medical diagnosis, clustering, image segmentation and decision-making.

Applications

A measure is only useful if it leads to better decisions, so each one was tested on concrete tasks. The clustering and image segmentation figures come from the papers; the others are sketches of how each task uses a distance or divergence.

Pattern recognition

Papers [1], [2], [4]

Each known pattern is a fuzzy set, and a new sample is assigned to the closest pattern. In paper [4], an unknown building material is classified into one of four known material classes. Almost all of the 24 operators we compared pick the same class, but ours separates it most clearly from the alternatives.

Medical diagnosis

Paper [2]

Patients and diseases are both described by symptoms, each with degrees of presence, absence and uncertainty. The diagnosis for a patient is the disease at the smallest distance.

Clustering

Papers [1], [2], [3], [4]

Given a reliable distance between every pair of objects, hierarchical clustering merges the most similar objects first, and cutting the tree at a chosen level gives the clusters. In paper [3], eight geographical environments, such as earthquake zones, coastal regions and deserts, are grouped by eight building-performance criteria.

Colour image segmentation

Paper [5]

Segmentation is clustering of pixels. Standard c-means methods treat every feature as equally important and use Euclidean distance, which is sensitive to noise. In paper [5] I developed four c-means methods that learn a weight for each feature, as a vector or a matrix, use a non-Euclidean distance, and constrain the product of the weights rather than their sum, so no extra parameters are needed. We tested them on 13 benchmark datasets and on colour photos, using a deep-learning segmentation (SegNet) as the reference.

Decision making

Papers [1], [3], [4]

When experts rate several alternatives on several criteria, often with hesitation, the divergence turns those ratings into a ranking. In paper [4], four blockchain platforms are ranked on six criteria. Our model ranks Solana first, which agrees with FFS-TOPSIS, a widely used method.

Conclusion

The lesson that runs through these papers is simple. Before using a measure of similarity or information, decide what properties it must have and prove that it has them. Measures built this way gave consistent answers in all five kinds of tasks, including the cases where older measures did not.

The same question shapes my current research. In single-cell representation learning I ask how much biological signal a learned representation actually recovers, which again comes down to choosing and justifying the right measure of information.
