Scaling Laws of Biological Information: From Single-Cell Representations to AI Models

Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health
Supervisors: Prof. Rong Ma and Prof. Allon Klein

The question

Single-cell sequencing reveals cellular diversity and changes in cell state across physiological conditions, disease, and experimental perturbations. To interpret these high-dimensional, noisy measurements, we turn them into representations of relationships among cells or genes, from linear projections to single-cell foundation models. The field keeps building larger atlases and perturbation screens, expecting that more data will reveal more biology. Yet how much biological information is gained by collecting more data, and when returns begin to diminish, remains difficult to predict. Across diverse representations, we observe systematic scaling of biological information recovery with cell number and molecular depth, but the rigorous theoretical foundations remain elusive.

A theory of single-cell scaling

Several factors act together. More cells improve estimation of shared expression patterns, while more UMIs per cell reduce molecular sampling noise. Batch structure mixes technical with biological variation, and the measurement quality required depends on the biological question. We develop a unified scaling-law framework based on random matrix theory that explains and predicts how biological information recovery scales with cell number, UMI depth, and technical batch structure. The framework characterizes recovery thresholds, scaling rates, and information ceilings, and it accounts for differences across representations and biological targets: cell populations, developmental trajectories, gene coexpression networks, and differential expression.

Scaling in AI pretraining

In machine learning, scaling laws that link performance to data, model size, and compute have guided model development and resource allocation. How they extend to single-cell foundation models remains open, since downstream gains can plateau after pretraining on only a fraction of the available data. We extend our framework to AI pretraining and to multimodal models that connect cellular representations with large language models. This shows how pretraining scale, model choice, and test-data quality shape biological information recovery, including in the generated text.

From scaling laws to experimental design

Information recovery also depends on how an experiment allocates its resources across cell number, sequencing depth, and technical batches. We extrapolate scaling laws fitted to pilot data to guide this choice. In a systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) study, we identify the cell-number and sequencing-depth combination that minimizes cost for a specified information target. In a perturbation screen, we maximize information recovery under a fixed budget, guiding the collection of training data for virtual-cell models.

Scaling laws turn “how much data is enough?” into a question that can be answered before the data are collected, providing a foundation for predicting biological information gains, evaluating representation quality, and guiding resource allocation across single-cell experiments and AI modeling.

Paper

Scaling Laws Predict How Much Biological Information Single-Cell Data Can Reveal. S. Zhu, J. Hu, G. Gowri, A. M. Klein, R. Ma. Submitted to Cell.
